The Lantern Keeper · Chapter I
Chapter One

The Keeper's Cottage

In which we arrive at the edge of Thornfield Moor
✦ ✦ ✦

The cottage stood at the far edge of Thornfield Moor, where the grass surrendered to heather and the wind carried the salt taste of the distant sea. It was a humble structure — whitewashed stone, a slate roof thick with moss, and a single chimney that drew the eye upward like a finger pointing to heaven. Around it, the moor stretched in every direction: vast, undulant, and the colour of old bronze in the late autumn light.

Marguerite Ashford arrived on the afternoon coach from Exeter, her travelling case balanced on her knees and a letter folded precisely into thirds within her breast pocket. The letter bore no return address, only a wax seal impressed with the image of a lantern, and inside, a single line written in an elegant, antiquated hand:

"The light must not go out."

She had read it thirty-seven times during the journey. Each reading offered no further clarity, yet each somehow deepened her conviction that she must come.

Fig. I — The approach to the Keeper's cottage across Thornfield Moor

The coachman refused to take her further than the crossroads at Wychburne. "Nothing good past those stones," he said, jerking his chin toward a pair of granite posts marking a track that wound into the heather. He did not elaborate, and the set of his jaw discouraged questions. Marguerite thanked him, collected her case, and stepped down into mud and drizzle.

The walk took forty minutes. The track was narrow and uncertain — more suggestion than path — weaving between thickets of gorse and stands of wind-bent hawthorn. Twice she lost her way entirely and had to orient herself by the distant chimney smoke, a thin grey thread that rose and dissolved into the low ceiling of cloud. By the time she reached the cottage door, her boots were ruined, her hair had escaped its pins, and she possessed the wild, windswept appearance of someone who had walked not merely across a moor but through a century.

The door opened before she knocked.

The Lantern Keeper · Chapter II
Chapter Two

The Old Man & the Flame

In which the lantern is revealed and a bargain is struck
✦ ✦ ✦

He was ancient. Not merely old in the way that people become old — the slow accretion of years that bends the spine and whitens the hair — but ancient in a manner that seemed geological. His face was the colour of walnut shell, creased with a thousand fine lines that mapped some unreadable territory. His eyes, set deep beneath brows like hedgerows, were the startling blue of a winter sky seen through ice.

"You received my letter," he said. It was not a question.

"I did." Marguerite stood on the threshold, rain dripping from her hat brim onto the flagstones. "Though I confess I don't understand it."

"Understanding comes later," the old man replied. "First comes the seeing." He stepped aside with a gesture that was less invitation than inevitability, and Marguerite crossed into the cottage.

Fig. II — The lantern upon the oak table, beside the Keeper's journal

The interior was a single room of surprising warmth. A fire burned in a hearth wide enough to roast a sheep, and above it hung a mantle crowded with curious objects: a compass with no markings on its dial, a bell without a clapper, three glass bottles containing what appeared to be different densities of darkness. The ceiling was low, the beams blackened by centuries of smoke, and from the central beam hung bundles of dried herbs whose names Marguerite could not guess.

But the room's true centre was the table. It was ancient oak, scarred and ring-stained, and upon it sat a lantern unlike any Marguerite had ever seen. It was perhaps eighteen inches tall, wrought of a dark metal that seemed to absorb light rather than reflect it. Its glass panels were faintly amber, and through them burned a flame of remarkable character: perfectly still, despite the draughts that crept through the cottage walls, and of a colour that shifted between gold and copper and, in certain moments, a deep and living blue.

"How long has it been burning?" Marguerite asked, drawn to the lantern as if by magnetism.

"Longer than this cottage. Longer than the moor. Longer than the name of the place where you now stand." The old man lowered himself into a chair by the fire with the careful deliberation of someone for whom each movement is a negotiation. "I have kept it for seventy-three years. My predecessor kept it for sixty-one. Before her, a man named Aubrey who lasted ninety years, though accounts suggest he was somewhat unusual."

The Compact

He explained it simply, as one might explain the rules of a card game. The lantern had been burning since before recorded history. No one knew who lit it or why, only that it must never be allowed to go out. It required no fuel — no oil, no wick, no tending in the conventional sense. What it required was a keeper: someone who would remain within sight of it, who would watch over its flame, and who would, when the time came, pass the duty to a successor.

"The flame asks for nothing but witness. It burns for those who remember what light means in the darkness."

"And if the flame goes out?" Marguerite heard herself ask.

The old man looked at her with those ice-blue eyes, and for the first time she saw in them something that might have been fear, or might have been reverence — the two emotions, she would later learn, being closer cousins than most people suppose.

"Then the darkness remembers what it was before the light."

Chapter Three

The First Night's Watch

In which the new keeper learns the weight of stillness
✦ ✦ ✦

Fig. III — The cottage window
at the midnight hour

Marguerite agreed. She could not have said precisely why, only that refusing felt impossible — not in the way of coercion, but in the way that breathing is impossible to refuse. Some things are simply what one does.

The old man departed the following morning, walking into the mist with the unhurried gait of someone who has finally set down a very heavy thing. He did not say goodbye. He simply was there, and then he was not, and the moor closed behind him like a curtain.

That first night alone with the lantern, Marguerite understood something that no explanation could have conveyed. The flame was alive. Not alive in the way of animals or plants, but alive in the way of music, or grief, or the memory of someone deeply loved. It shifted and breathed and, in the deep silence of the moor at midnight, it seemed almost to pulse in rhythm with her heartbeat.

She sat in the old man's chair — her chair now — and watched the flame, and the flame watched her, and the cottage held them both in its ancient, creaking arms, and outside the wind sang across the heather in a language older than words.

The Hours Between Midnight and Dawn

In the weeks that followed, Marguerite established a routine that would have seemed intolerable to her former self. She rose at dawn, tended the small garden behind the cottage (potatoes, kale, herbs whose names she learned from a hand-drawn guide left in the kitchen drawer), and spent the daylight hours reading from the Keeper's library — a collection of extraordinary breadth, ranging from astronomical treatises to volumes of poetry in languages she did not recognise.

But the nights belonged to the lantern. Each evening as darkness gathered, she would draw her chair close to the oak table, light a tallow candle for her own reading, and settle into what she came to think of as "the watch." Nothing happened during these vigils. That was precisely the point. She was learning the discipline of attention, the art of being present to something that asked nothing of her except presence itself.

And slowly, in that silence and stillness, she began to hear what the flame was saying.

A Note from the Author

This excerpt is drawn from a longer work that took shape over several years, much of it written in a small cottage not entirely unlike the one described in these pages. The Lantern Keeper began as a meditation on solitude and became, as stories often do, something else entirely — a reflection on what it means to tend a light that was not ours to kindle and will not be ours to extinguish.

I am indebted to the moorlands of Devon and Cornwall, which lent their weather and their silence to these pages, and to the memory of my grandmother, who understood better than anyone the sacred work of keeping watch.

— Eleanor Vance, Dartmoor, 1948
